# Persona 3: Rohan, simulated customer discovery interview

**Status:** Synthetic. Rohan is an invented persona used to pressure-test the Nope concept before real interviews. Nothing below is real customer data, and no statistic in this file is sourced. Any factual claim about a competitor, platform or permission is marked (unverified).

**Product tested:** Nope. A small round NFC puck plus an Android app (iPhone version later, dependent on Apple's Screen Time API). Tap phone on puck to lock chosen apps, tap again to unlock. Two emergency unlocks per day, streaks, daily focus time. No subscription.

**Persona:** Rohan, 28, software engineer in Bengaluru. Works from home three days a week. Doomscrolls X, YouTube, LinkedIn and Instagram between tasks. Has tried Screen Time, Digital Wellbeing, Forest and Freedom. Uses an iPhone 15 personally and an Android work phone. Reads product reviews carefully.

**Interviewer:** I | **Rohan:** R

---

## Transcript

**I1. Walk me through a normal workday on your phones.**

R: I open the personal iPhone before standup, mostly LinkedIn, because that's where I see what people shipped overnight. The work Android sits on my desk all day. On WFH days I bounce every 20 minutes between Slack, a build, X and back. The personal phone is face down next to the keyboard, and it's closer to my hand than I'd like to admit.

**I1a. How much of that is leisure?**

R: Honestly, most of the gaps. I'll open Instagram to check one message and come out 25 minutes later watching a cricket highlight I don't even care about.

**I2. What have you tried to cut down?**

R: Screen Time on the iPhone, then Digital Wellbeing on the Android. I set Instagram to 30 minutes a day. Then the reminder pops up and I tap "Ignore limit for today." Done. I once asked my sister to set a Screen Time passcode. She used my birthday. I'm a software engineer and I still got around it, which is embarrassing.

**I3. Why did those stop working?**

R: The friction is wrong. Tapping a button to ignore is zero effort. Also the limits are per app. I hit the Instagram limit and open X instead. I need the whole category blocked, not one app.

**I4. Tell me about Forest and Freedom.**

R: Forest was fun for about a month. I grew trees, then realised I was growing trees on my phone, which is the exact problem. Freedom blocks sites across devices, which I liked. But it's a software block, and when a deadline hit I just turned it off. It only blocks when I let it. I think it can also be set up with a schedule, but I never got it to stick (unverified, I have not rechecked).

**I5. Where is the line between work and leisure? What must stay open?**

R: Slack, Gmail, Google Calendar, Meet, and the build tools obviously. WhatsApp is non-negotiable because my mother and the building society group are on it. Maps and UPI must stay. If you lock UPI on a Tuesday when I'm at a chai stall, you've made an enemy for life. So lock the leisure list, never the utility list.

**I6. How do you feel about a physical puck?**

R: I like it, because a dedicated object means I have to walk to it, and that's the friction I want. But I have a drawer of smart-home things that died. A battery ran out, an app stopped updating. I'd want no battery, or at least a year of life. And honestly I'd put it in the kitchen and forget where it is.

**I7. You use an iPhone and an Android work phone. Which would you use Nope on?**

R: The iPhone is the one I doom-scroll on, so an Android-only launch is a real problem. I'd buy it for the work phone, but the work phone is the one I need less. I'd wait for iPhone. Also, my work phone is managed by company policy, so I'm not sure IT would let me install it at all (unverified, I assume).

**I8. Here are four prices: Rs 1,999, Rs 3,499, Rs 5,700 and Rs 7,500. React to each.**

R: Rs 1,999: my first thought is what's cheap about it. An unknown NFC puck at that price on Amazon, I'd expect it to peel or stop working in six months. Rs 3,499: that's a number I'd buy after a week of reading reviews. Rs 5,700: I think Brick is around that, I'd check it, and I'd compare them side by side. Unless yours is clearly better in design or software, it's a coin flip and I'd pick the one with more reviews. Rs 7,500: I need a strong reason. I did spend a lot on a keyboard last year and it's the best thing on my desk, but a phone-lock puck would have to justify itself much more than that.

**I9. What does premium mean to you?**

R: The tap feels solid, the puck is heavy enough not to slide on the desk, the app doesn't ask for fourteen permissions, and the box doesn't look like a cheap gift. I'm not paying for a logo. Though I'll admit I bought a Rs 1,200 charging stand last year because it looked good, and it still rattles.

**I10. The Android version needs an accessibility-style permission. How do you feel?**

R: I'd stop right there. I know what accessibility services can read on screen. On a work phone I'd get a call from IT. As an engineer I'd want the exact permission name and a clear list of what the app can and cannot see. If it can see what's on screen to block apps, what else can it see? I'd much prefer it used the system's own app-blocking mechanism if one exists (unverified which Android mechanism applies).

**I11. Subscriptions?**

R: I already pay for Notion, a VPN, a password manager and YouTube Premium. I cancel anything that asks monthly for a utility. "No subscription" would make me buy faster. But I'd also wonder how a free forever product makes money, and whether the company will still exist in two years.

**I12. How would you get around it?**

R: Easy, honestly. I'd put the puck in a cupboard, or leave my phone in the bedroom and use the laptop for X. Or I'd make a second account. Or I'd tap the puck with my sister's phone. And the emergency unlock: two a day is too many. I'd burn both in the first hour on Monday morning and feel sad about it.

**I13. What would make it a daily habit, and what would make you stop?**

R: The streak only works if I can see it. If the focus time is on the lock screen or a widget, I'd check it. Streaks feel childish after a while, though. What would keep me is a Sunday summary of hours I got back, which I could compare with my own guess. What would make me stop: any nag notification, a tap that fails when I'm late for a call, or a lock that traps me during something urgent.

**I14. Would you buy one as a gift?**

R: Yes, for my father, who spends evenings on YouTube Shorts. I'd include a note saying "try this, not a lecture." I wouldn't give one to my younger cousin in college, because she'd read it as her parents sending her a message. And I'd only gift it if I could set it up for them first.

**I15. Which features or integrations would you pay extra for?**

R: Calendar sync, so meetings lock automatically: yes. Focus schedules, like 9 to 12 locked every weekday, set once: yes. Work mode is the one I'd pay the most for, with Slack, Gmail, Meet and the IDE allowed by default. I'd pay a one-time add-on fine. A monthly one I'd resent.

**I16. Anything I should have asked?**

R: Ask who the company is. Gadget startups die, and I want to know who replaces a broken puck and what happens to my data if you shut down. Also, can I lock only at night? That's the time I actually lose.

## Analysis

### Top 5 pains

1. **Willpower tools are too easy to dismiss.** Screen Time's "ignore limit" and a guessed passcode defeated the tool in minutes. The bypass costs one tap or one guess.
2. **Per-app limits leak.** Rohan substitutes X for Instagram. He wants category-level blocks (social, video, shopping).
3. **The phone is always within reach.** His distraction is physical: the phone sits near the keyboard. Distance and deliberate walking are the friction he describes.
4. **Fear of breaking utilities.** UPI, WhatsApp, Maps, calendar and work tools must stay open. Any lock that catches a utility creates resentment and likely uninstalls.
5. **Gadget fatigue.** Dead batteries and unmaintained apps have burned him. He wants durable hardware with no dependency on a live service.

### Top 5 objections

1. **Android only.** His personal iPhone is the main doomscroll device, so the launch misses where his distraction actually lives.
2. **Accessibility-style permission on a work phone.** Privacy and corporate IT policy are both trust blockers. He wants a precise, verifiable explanation.
3. **Easy bypass.** Move the puck, use another device, create a second account. He will tell you this himself.
4. **Price anchoring against Brick.** He compares Nope to Brick at Rs 5,700 and reads price as a quality signal. Without a visible difference, he defaults to the product with more reviews.
5. **Business model and company survival.** "No subscription" raises the question of how the company earns money, and whether a hardware startup will last.

### Price answer

**Recommended test anchor for launch: Rs 3,499.** Rejected: Rs 1,999 (reads as a quality warning) and Rs 7,500 (needs proof of value Nope has not shown). Rs 5,700 works only if the design, Work mode or durability clearly beat Brick. That comparison is not established yet, and Brick's price is itself unverified (Rohan was unsure of it).

Reasoning from this interview only:
- Below roughly Rs 2,000, the product reads as untrustworthy for him.
- Around Rs 3,499 he is ready to buy after research, provided reviews back it up.
- Above Rs 5,000 he starts comparing against named alternatives, so the price must be justified in writing.

This is one synthetic persona. It is a hypothesis about the price band, not a market price. Validate with a landing-page price test across Rs 3,499 and Rs 3,999 before committing.

### Buying triggers

- A demo where the tap-to-lock takes about two seconds and the phone visibly locks.
- Reviews from people describing the same pain, especially on Work mode and Slack compatibility.
- A clear iPhone timeline, or a plain statement of what works on iPhone in the meantime.
- Gift framing with a setup card, so the giver can configure it for the recipient.
- A clear answer on company continuity and what happens to data on shutdown.
- A return window, which lowers the risk of a Rs 3,499 hardware purchase.

### Churn risks

- Emergency unlock is abused, and the user loses trust in week one.
- Over-blocking catches a utility during a call, a payment or an urgent message.
- Streaks feel childish and novelty fades within weeks.
- The puck is lost, moved or broken, with no quick recovery path.
- A reviewer or security post raises the accessibility permission, and the user uninstalls.
- Battery or durability failure in the puck, which he has already been burned by elsewhere.

### 5 concrete recommendations

1. **Lead with Work mode and calendar auto-lock.** These are the features Rohan would pay for. Make them the headline in the app and the landing page, not an afterthought.
2. **Block by category, with a locked-in "utilities always on" list.** Pre-fill UPI, Maps, Calendar, WhatsApp and work tools so users cannot accidentally lock them. Let users remove items, not add them by mistake.
3. **Tighten emergency unlock.** Test one unlock per day with a short wait (for example, a 10-minute delay), or two with a cooldown. Show the user what each unlock costs, such as the streak count. Two unlocks a day is likely to be burned on day one.
4. **Publish a plain-language permissions page.** State the exact Android permission, what the app can and cannot read, and that it works offline if true. Commission an independent security review before launch (the need for one is an assumption, unverified).
5. **Price at Rs 3,499 with a visible comparison page.** Show what Nope does that the cheaper and more expensive alternatives do not, with materials and durability specs in a review-friendly format. Offer a 30-day return and say "no subscription" in the first line of copy, since it is a real differentiator for him.

### Assumptions to validate with real people

- Rohan is representative. This is one synthetic persona. Run 15 to 20 real interviews across iPhone and Android users in India, including students and non-engineers.
- The Rs 3,499 band holds for non-engineers, and the Rs 1,999 "quality warning" reaction is real and not specific to Rohan.
- The Android permission required, and whether corporate IT will allow it, is acceptable to users (unverified).
- Apple's Screen Time API supports the planned lock behaviour and the approval timeline is workable (unverified).
- Category-level blocking is preferred over per-app blocking.
- Brick's price and feature set are as stated in the brief (unverified, Rohan was unsure).
- Users value a tap-based physical lock enough to keep it in use after week one.
- A no-subscription hardware model can be sustained financially.
- Gift buyers are a separate segment and will pay for a setup card.
- Emergency unlock count of two is not too generous (unverified, based only on Rohan's reaction).
