# Psychology and Copywriting Craft of Cold Messages (Job-Seeker Outreach)

Scope note: evidence is labelled by strength. [PEER-REVIEWED] = journal research; [LARGE-SAMPLE VENDOR DATA] = platform/vendor analyses (big N, but not peer-reviewed, sales-email context, and vendors have commercial incentives); [PRACTITIONER OPINION] = copywriting authorities' reasoning, not tested. Several primary pages (psypost, news.ufl.edu, lavender.ai, the-decoder) were blocked by the network proxy, so some findings rely on search-result summaries of those pages; this is noted inline.

## 1. Which psychological levers are supported by research for getting help from strangers?

### Takeaway
The best-supported levers are: (a) simply asking directly and specifically (people say yes far more than askers predict), (b) genuine, uncommon similarity with the asker, (c) framing the ask as advice rather than a favour, and (d) small, easy first requests. Reciprocity and curiosity are real but weaker or harder to apply in a first message from a job seeker; foot-in-the-door and the Ben Franklin effect are smaller/less replicated than pop-psychology suggests.

### Cited Findings
- **Askers underestimate compliance.** [PEER-REVIEWED] Flynn & Lake (2008, JPSP 95(1):128-143): across three studies (borrowing a stranger's phone, filling in a questionnaire, walking a student to the campus gym), people underestimated by as much as 50% the likelihood that strangers would agree to a direct request; they overestimated by ~50% how many people they'd need to ask. Cause: askers focus on the helper's cost of saying yes and neglect the social cost of saying no. — [Bohns/Flynn & Lake PDF, Cornell eCommons](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/bitstream/handle/1813/75652/Bohns14_If_You_Need_Help_Just_Ask.pdf;jsessionid=47B3D596042182750D973E147A106704?sequence=1); [Stanford GSB summary](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/news/research/flynn_ask.html)
- **But the "cost of saying no" is weakest in text.** [PEER-REVIEWED] Roghanizad & Bohns (2017): 495 students each asked 10 strangers to complete a survey, randomly assigned to in-person vs email; in-person was far more effective and email requesters overestimated how persuasive their emails would be. (The widely quoted "34x more effective" figure is from Bohns' HBR write-up title; I could not verify the number from a fetched source.) — [Entrepreneur summary](https://www.entrepreneur.com/article/293437)
- **Incidental similarity raises compliance, but only if it is uncommon and with the asker.** [PEER-REVIEWED] Burger et al. (2004, PSPB): students who believed they shared a birthday, first name, or fingerprint type with a requester were more likely to agree to a costly request (read an 8-page essay and write a page of feedback by the next day). No effect when the similarity was with someone other than the requester, or when the shared feature was believed to be common. — [Baylor summary](https://kellercenter.hankamer.baylor.edu/news/story/2010/persuasive-role-incidental-similarity-purchase-intentions); [bakadesuyo summary](https://bakadesuyo.com/2011/09/whats-an-easy-way-to-get-others-to-agree-to-h/)
- **Shared connections/company/groups lift InMail response.** [LARGE-SAMPLE VENDOR DATA, LinkedIn's own InMail data as reported] Mentioning a mutual connection or shared company is reported to raise reply rate ~27%; InMails to shared-group members got ~21% higher response; personalizing with common groups/experiences ~10%. — [Salesflow summary of LinkedIn data](https://salesflow.io/blog/linkedin-inmail-best-practices-improve-response-rates); [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-shares-new-insights-into-maximize-response-to-your-inmail-messages/624430)
- **Advice-seeking boosts perceived competence of the asker.** [PEER-REVIEWED] Brooks, Gino & Schweitzer (2015, Management Science 61(6):1421-1435): people fear asking makes them look incompetent, but advisors rate advice-seekers as *more* competent. Effect is strongest when the task is difficult and when the person asked is (and sees themselves as) an expert in that area; it works partly by flattering the advisor's self-image. — [IDEAS/RePEc listing](https://ideas.repec.org/r/inm/ormnsc/v61y2015i6p1421-1435.html); [SUFE newsletter summary](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/393572); [Univ. Mannheim summary](https://www.uni-mannheim.de/forschung-erleben/artikel/wer-mich-um-rat-fragt-ist-kompetent/)
- **Foot-in-the-door is real but small.** [PEER-REVIEWED meta-analysis] Meta-analysis of FITD and door-in-the-face found both small (r = .17 and .15), even under optimal conditions; the original Freedman & Fraser magnitude has never been replicated. — [Penn State Pure: sequential-request meta-analysis](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/sequentialrequest-persuasive-strategies-metaanalysis-of-footinthe/); [replication-crisis summary](https://www.atticusli.com/replication-crisis/foot-in-the-door/); [Poland/Ukraine direct replication collection](https://sage.figshare.com/collections/The_Foot-in-the-Door_Phenomenon_40_and_50_Years_Later_A_Direct_Replication_of_the_Original_Freedman_and_Fraser_Study_in_Poland_and_in_Ukraine/4653512)
- **Ben Franklin effect rests on thin evidence.** [PEER-REVIEWED but old/small] Jecker & Landy (1969): participants asked by the researcher personally to return winnings liked him more than those asked by a secretary. I found little on modern replications. — [Wikipedia: Ben Franklin effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_Franklin_effect)
- **Reciprocity is powerful but needs a genuine prior gift.** [PEER-REVIEWED, classic] Regan (1971): a confederate who gave an unsolicited 10-cent Coke later sold about twice as many raffle tickets; the favour drove compliance regardless of how much the target liked the confederate. — [summary](https://clawhub.ai/deciqai/skills/reciprocity) (secondary source; the original is Regan 1971, J. Exp. Soc. Psych.)
- **Curiosity = information gap, and needs some prior knowledge.** [PEER-REVIEWED theory] Loewenstein (1994): curiosity arises when attention is focused on a gap in what one knows; it requires some initial knowledge. — [PBS NOVA](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/how-to-stimulate-curiosity/); [Nautilus](https://nautil.us/curiosity-depends-on-what-you-already-know-235803)
- **Weak ties do carry jobs.** [PEER-REVIEWED, very large experiment] Rajkumar et al. (2022, Science): randomized experiments on LinkedIn's PYMK across 20M+ people over 5 years (2B new ties, 600k new jobs). Moderately weak ties (few mutual connections) produced the most job mobility; effects were nonlinear; weak ties mattered more in digital industries, strong ties in less digital ones. — [MIT IDE](https://ide.mit.edu/insights/new-publication-mit-harvard-and-stanford-scientists-show-weaker-ties-are-more-beneficial-for-job-seekers-on-linkedin/); [Gwern PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/sociology/technology/2022-rajkumar.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a job seeker: lead with a *specific, real, non-trivial* commonality (same small program, same previous employer team, same hometown, same niche project), not a broad one ("we both work in tech"). Burger's "common similarity has no effect" result predicts that generic similarity does nothing.
- Frame the first ask as a narrow, expert-matched advice question ("How did you decide between X and Y when you moved into [team]?") rather than "Can you refer me?". This uses advice-seeking (flatters expertise, makes asker look competent) and keeps the request small (FITD, even if weak).
- Because email/DM drops the social cost of saying no, the message must make yes cheap: one question, answerable in a sentence or two, no reading required.
- Reciprocity is hard for a job seeker to deploy honestly in message one; a genuine small give (a relevant note on their post, a useful pointer) can work, but contrived "value adds" risk reading as sales tactics.
- Curiosity-gap hooks from sales copy ("I noticed something about your team...") are risky for job seekers; they read as bait. A concrete observation creates a smaller, honest gap.
- Targeting second/third-degree contacts (moderately weak ties) is supported by Rajkumar et al.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found that directly tests job-seeker cold messages to employees (referral requests) on LinkedIn/email.
- Could not verify the Yoon/Blunden/Kristal/Whillans "advice vs feedback" study or the exact "34x" figure from a primary source.
- No modern replication data found for the Ben Franklin effect.

## 2. How do hooks and opening lines work best?

### Takeaway
Evidence favours short, individually written messages that open with something specific to the recipient (shared path, a recent post or move, a concrete project) and contain one clear, easy question. Multiple questions and long messages lower replies. Observation-then-question is the dominant practitioner pattern.

### Cited Findings
- **Short wins.** [LARGE-SAMPLE VENDOR DATA] LinkedIn InMail data: messages of 400 characters or fewer had ~22% higher response than average; up to 800 still +5%; over 1,200 characters ~11% below average. — [Salesflow summary](https://salesflow.io/blog/linkedin-inmail-best-practices-improve-response-rates); [Search Engine Journal](https://searchenginejournal.com/linkedin-data-reveals-what-type-of-inmail-gets-best-results/451007)
- **Individually sent beats bulk.** [LARGE-SAMPLE VENDOR DATA] InMails sent individually see ~15% higher response than bulk InMails. — [Salesflow summary](https://salesflow.io/blog/linkedin-inmail-best-practices-improve-response-rates)
- **Very short emails do best.** [LARGE-SAMPLE VENDOR DATA] Hunter analysis of ~34M cold emails: 20-39 words had the highest average reply rate. — [Hunter](https://hunter.io/blog/cold-email-word-count)
- **Fewer questions.** [VENDOR DATA] Reply.io: emails with no questions got 11% higher reply rate and 25% higher "interested" rate; three questions cut reply and interest by up to 21%. — [Reply.io research PDF](https://reply.io/wp-content/uploads/How-to-write-better-cold-emails-Reply-Research.pdf) (via search summary). Note: this is sales data where "no question" usually means a soft statement-CTA; one question is still the norm in practitioner advice.
- **Simple reading level.** [VENDOR DATA / PRACTITIONER] Lavender recommends a grade 3-5 reading level; its benchmark of 231,818 cold emails reports that A-graded emails lifted reply rates from 3.4% to 4.3% (+27%). Lavender scores length, reading level, personalization, CTA, question count/position and tone. — [Woodpecker on Lavender](https://woodpecker.co/blog/lavender-ai/); [Lavender benchmark report](https://www.lavender.ai/blog/the-cold-email-benchmark-report) (page blocked; figures from search summary)
- **Observation/question over pitch.** [PRACTITIONER OPINION] Josh Braun: avoid "commission breath"; replace pitching with a neutral "illumination" question that mirrors the recipient's situation (e.g. "How are you ensuring X?" rather than "I can fix X"). The goal is to create a moment of reflection, not push. — [befreed summary of Braun](https://www.befreed.ai/podcast/psychology-of-cold-outreach); [Braun course page](https://joshbraun.teachable.com/courses/poke/lectures/47070266)

### Inferences
- Recommended structure for a job seeker's first message (all in ~50-90 words / under 400 characters on LinkedIn):
  1. A specific hook proving this message is for this person only: shared path ("I'm also a [small program] grad and saw you moved from [shared ex-employer] to [Company]'s payments team") or a concrete reaction to a recent post/talk/launch (naming the specific point, not "great post").
  2. One sentence of who you are, relevant to them.
  3. One narrow advice question they can answer from memory.
  No link, no CV attachment, no ask for a call in message one.
- Observation beats question as the opener; the question belongs at the end. Several questions dilute and raise effort.
- Referencing a recent post works if the reference is substantive (quote or engage a specific idea) and recent (practitioner norm: days/weeks, not years).

### Gaps
- No controlled test found comparing "shared path" vs "recent post" hooks for job seekers specifically.
- Vendor data is from sales outreach; job-seeker messages ask for less and may get higher baseline replies.

## 3. What makes personalization feel genuine vs creepy or flattering?

### Takeaway
Flattery can work implicitly even when recognised as self-serving, but explicit evaluations discount it, and generic compliments are a template signal. Genuine personalization is specific, verifiable, relevant to the ask, and drawn from what the person chose to publish professionally.

### Cited Findings
- **Insincere flattery still leaves a positive implicit trace.** [PEER-REVIEWED] Chan & Sengupta (2010, Journal of Marketing Research 47(1)): even when targets saw an obvious ulterior motive and discounted the compliment explicitly, a favourable implicit attitude persisted and predicted later behaviour more than the explicit attitude. — [HKUST summary](https://bm.hkust.edu.hk/bizinsight/2010/03/insincere-flattery-works); [AMA research insight](https://www.ama.org/research-insights/research-insight-even-phony-flattery-helps-close-deals/)
- **Advice-seeking flatters without overt compliments.** [PEER-REVIEWED] Brooks et al. (2015): the competence boost operates partly by flattering the advisor's self-perception as an expert. — [SUFE summary](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/393572)
- **Common/generic similarity does nothing.** [PEER-REVIEWED] Burger et al. (2004): no compliance boost when the shared feature was believed common. — [Baylor summary](https://kellercenter.hankamer.baylor.edu/news/story/2010/persuasive-role-incidental-similarity-purchase-intentions)
- **Sent individually beats bulk** (the recipient can partly sense mass sending). [VENDOR DATA] ~15% higher. — [Salesflow summary](https://salesflow.io/blog/linkedin-inmail-best-practices-improve-response-rates)

### Inferences
- Chan & Sengupta suggests flattery isn't toxic, but a job seeker is judged explicitly (a reasoned decision to reply), where discounting applies. Better to show respect by asking for expertise (implicit flattery) than to compliment ("Your impressive career...").
- Rubric for genuine vs creepy: genuine = specific, about professional output they published, tied to why you're writing. Creepy = personal-life details, deep-dug info (old posts from years ago, family), stacking several facts to show research effort. Flattering/generic = adjectives with no content ("inspiring journey", "impressive background").
- Test: "Could this sentence be sent unchanged to 50 other people?" If yes, it's a template signal, not personalization.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found on the "creepiness threshold" of personalization in professional cold messages; the guidance above is inference plus practitioner norms.

## 4. Spam and template signals recipients associate with mass outreach

### Takeaway
The clearest evidence-backed signals are length, bulk-sending, multiple questions, and generic openings. Specific phrase lists ("I came across your profile", "pick your brain") are mostly practitioner consensus rather than measured; surveys of annoying email phrases focus on follow-up clichés.

### Cited Findings
- **Annoying phrase surveys.** [SURVEY] Perkbox (2020): "Just looping in" (37% dislike), "as per my last email" (33%), "any updates on this" (24%), "just checking in" (19%). Adobe: "Not sure if you saw my last email" was most irritating (25%). "Hope you're well" ranked lower (~6% annoyed). — [BusinessTech](https://businesstech.co.za/news/internet/374302/are-these-the-most-annoying-phrases-you-can-use-in-an-email/); [ITPro](https://itpro.com/email-delivery/31789/these-are-officially-the-most-hated-email-phrases)
- **Length and bulk lower response.** [VENDOR DATA] >1,200-character InMails ~11% below average; bulk sends ~15% lower than individual. — [Salesflow summary](https://salesflow.io/blog/linkedin-inmail-best-practices-improve-response-rates)
- **Multiple questions lower replies.** [VENDOR DATA] Up to 21% drop with three questions. — [Reply.io PDF](https://reply.io/wp-content/uploads/How-to-write-better-cold-emails-Reply-Research.pdf)
- **"Commission breath" (visible self-interest) repels.** [PRACTITIONER OPINION] Braun. — [befreed summary](https://www.befreed.ai/podcast/psychology-of-cold-outreach)

### Inferences (ban list for writer guidance; these are practitioner consensus unless sourced above)
- Openers: "I came across your profile", "I hope this message finds you well", "I hope you're doing well", "My name is X and I...", "I wanted to reach out", "I'm reaching out because".
- Asks: "pick your brain", "hop on a quick call", "15 minutes of your time", "I'd love to connect", "any opportunities", "Can you refer me?" in message one, "Let me know if you have any openings".
- Follow-ups: "just checking in", "bumping this up", "not sure if you saw my last message", "following up on my previous email", "as per my last".
- Structure: more than ~120 words / 3 short paragraphs; several questions; bullet lists of your achievements; résumé paste; links or attachments in message one; subject lines in Title Case or with "Opportunity"/"Quick question"; emojis; exclamation marks; bracket-style merge artefacts ("Hi [Name]"), wrong name or company.
- Content: generic praise ("impressive profile", "inspiring journey"); talking about yourself before them; vague ask ("any advice").

### Gaps
- No large-sample study found measuring reply impact of specific openers like "I came across your profile" or "pick your brain"; this is practitioner opinion.
- No reliable data found on emojis or links in job-seeker LinkedIn messages specifically.

## 5. AI-written text tells (2025-2026) and how disclosure/detection affects trust

### Takeaway
Recipients mostly cannot detect AI-written short personal messages and rate them fine when unaware, but when they know or suspect AI, they judge the sender as lazy, insincere and less caring, especially in relational messages. Because cold outreach is relational and recipients increasingly scan for AI tells, a message that *looks* AI-written risks the full disclosure penalty. Known tells: em dashes, stock vocabulary ("delve", "showcase", "underscore"), and over-polished generic tone.

### Cited Findings
- **Undetected AI is not penalised; disclosed AI is.** [PEER-REVIEWED/preprint, N>1,300] Zhu & Molnar (2025, Univ. of Michigan), "Blissful (A)Ignorance": with no authorship info, participants did not detect AI and formed impressions as positive as for human-written; when told the message was AI-written they judged senders more negatively ("lazy", "insincere"). Frequent AI users penalised slightly less but weren't more suspicious by default. — [U-M Psychology news](https://prod.lsa.umich.edu/psych/news-events/all-news/faculty-news/most-people-do-not-realize-when-a-personal-message-they-receive-.html); [PsyPost (search summary)](https://www.psypost.org/people-remain-blissfully-ignorant-of-ai-use-in-everyday-messages-new-research-shows/); [Let's Data Science](https://letsdatascience.com/news/study-shows-people-miss-ai-written-personal-messages-33e1d2ed)
- **AI help signals low effort and harms relationships.** [PEER-REVIEWED] Liu et al. (Ohio State, Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, 2023, N=208): a friend who used AI to write a message was seen as putting in less effort; recipients felt less satisfied and more uncertain about the relationship. People felt no third party (AI or human) should help write relational messages. — [Ohio State News](https://news.osu.edu/ai-can-help-write-a-message-to-a-friend--but-dont-do-it/)
- **Professional context: polished but less trustworthy.** [PEER-REVIEWED] Cardon & Coman (2025, International Journal of Business Communication, ~1,100 professionals): AI-assisted supervisor emails were rated clear, efficient and professional, but trust-related perceptions (sincerity, caring, authenticity) fell as AI assistance rose; AI was more accepted for transactional than relational messages. (Exact percentages could not be verified; news.ufl.edu blocked.) — [HR Reporter](https://www.hrreporter.com/focus-areas/automation-ai/using-ai-for-internal-communications-know-the-risks/393831); [UF Warrington](https://warrington.ufl.edu/news/should-you-use-ai-to-write-work-emails)
- **Belief in AI authorship lowers ratings of identical text.** [PEER-REVIEWED, N=2,587, as reported] Readers rated AI-written stories highly and could not reliably identify authorship, but gave higher ratings when they believed a person wrote them. — [Earth.com](https://www.earth.com/news/readers-prefer-ai-generated-stories/)
- **Vocabulary tells are measurable.** [PEER-REVIEWED] Kobak et al. (Science Advances, July 2025): 14M PubMed abstracts; "delves" appeared ~25x more in 2024 than pre-LLM trend predicted; "showcasing" and "underscores" ~9x; at least 10% of 2024 abstracts LLM-processed. — [arXiv 2406.07016](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07016v5)
- **Em dash as a popular tell.** [OPINION/CULTURAL] Since about October 2024, recruiters, tech workers and marketers on Reddit/LinkedIn have pointed to em dashes as a shortcut for spotting AI text; some argue newer models have reduced it. — [Yahoo Tech](https://tech.yahoo.com/ai/articles/em-dashes-really-sign-ai-162348067.html); [Every](https://every.to/learning-curve/what-em-dashes-say-about-ai-writing-and-us); [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/ai-platforms-assistants/chatgpt/the-days-of-the-em-dash-being-a-chatgpt-giveaway-are-over-its-time-to-bring-it-back)
- **Founder/investor reaction.** [OPINION] Paul Graham (Y Combinator) has said AI-written founder emails feel like being lied to. — [The Decoder headline](https://the-decoder.com/y-combinator-founder-paul-graham-says-ai-written-founder-emails-feel-like-being-lied-to/) (page blocked; exact quote and date not verified)

### Inferences (AI-tell ban list for the writer and rubric)
- Ban: em dashes (and spaced en dashes used as em dashes); "delve", "showcase", "underscore", "testament", "tapestry", "navigate the landscape", "in today's fast-paced world", "I hope this message finds you well", "I'm passionate about", "resonated with me", "leverage", "synergy", "excited to", "thrilled"; tricolons/lists of three adjectives; "not only X but also Y"; closing "Looking forward to hearing from you!"; perfectly parallel sentence lengths; summary sentences restating the message.
- Prefer: plain words, contractions, one slightly informal clause, a concrete detail only the sender could know, uneven sentence lengths, no headers or bullet points.
- Rubric implication: because the penalty is triggered by *suspected* AI, "would a sceptical reader suspect AI?" should be a scored criterion independent of quality. Polish is not a goal; effort signals and specificity are.

### Gaps
- No study found that measures reply rates to cold outreach judged as AI-written vs human (only impression/trust studies).
- The em-dash tell is cultural observation, not measured in a detection study I could find.

## 6. Transitioning from rapport to an ask (referral, intro, hiring manager name); timing and follow-ups

### Takeaway
Research supports making requests direct, specific and low-cost, and escalating gradually (small first, larger later), but sequential-request effects are small, so each step must stand on its own merit. Follow-up data shows one or two polite follow-ups substantially raise total replies, with sharp diminishing returns and rising complaints after about four.

### Cited Findings
- **Direct asks succeed more than askers expect.** [PEER-REVIEWED] Flynn & Lake (2008). — [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/news/research/flynn_ask.html)
- **Sequential requests (small then large) have small effects.** [PEER-REVIEWED meta-analysis] r ≈ .17 for FITD. — [Penn State Pure](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/sequentialrequest-persuasive-strategies-metaanalysis-of-footinthe/)
- **Follow-ups add replies.** [VENDOR DATA] Woodpecker (20M+ emails): one follow-up raised average reply from ~9% to ~13%. Belkins (16.5M emails, 2025): first follow-up increased replies up to 49% in high-performing campaigns; reports ~60% of replies arrive after the first follow-up; after a fourth follow-up, unsubscribe and spam complaints more than triple. Note: Belkins also reports the highest single-message reply rate (8.4%) for one-email sequences, so its figures are partly in tension; treat as directional. — [Instantly summary](https://instantly.ai/blog/follow-up-emails-vs-no-followup/); [Belkins](https://belkins.io/blog/sales-follow-up-statistics)
- **Follow-up clichés annoy.** [SURVEY] "Just checking in", "not sure if you saw my last email", "any updates" are top-disliked phrases. — [BusinessTech](https://businesstech.co.za/news/internet/374302/are-these-the-most-annoying-phrases-you-can-use-in-an-email/)

### Inferences
- Sequence for a job seeker: (1) message one = hook + one advice question; (2) after they reply, thank them specifically for what they said and reflect it back, maybe one follow-up question; (3) then a specific, easy-to-decline ask, explicitly tied to their advice: "Given what you said about the platform team, would you be open to referring me for [Req ID / role link], or pointing me to the hiring manager? Totally fine if not." Specificity (role, ID) reduces their effort; an explicit out reduces the social pressure that otherwise breeds resentment.
- Don't ask for a referral in message one to a stranger: it's a large favour that risks the person's reputation and gives no basis for trust. Asking for the hiring manager's name or "who'd be the right person to ask about X" is a smaller middle step.
- Follow-up cadence: one follow-up after ~3-7 days that adds something new (a new angle, a short relevant update), never "just checking in"; maximum two follow-ups total for a job seeker (the sales threshold of four is too aggressive for a personal ask). (Specific day counts are practitioner norms, not evidence-backed here.)
- If they don't reply to the advice question, don't escalate to a referral ask in a follow-up.

### Gaps
- No research found on the optimal number of exchanges before a referral ask, or on day-of-week/time-of-day for job-seeker messages.
- No job-seeker-specific follow-up data; all follow-up stats are B2B sales email.
